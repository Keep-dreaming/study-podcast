Part one. Wired connections.

There are four connectors to know.

The first is USB-C. It's small, oval, and reversible, so it plugs in either way up. Most new phones, tablets, and laptops use it. One USB-C cable can carry data, power, and even video to a monitor. It can deliver enough power to charge a laptop. And Thunderbolt uses the same connector.

The second is micro USB. It's thin, with a tapered shape, so it only fits one way. You'll find it on older Android phones, and on lots of cheap accessories.

The third is mini USB. It's older and a bit larger than micro USB. Think older digital cameras, and old game controllers.

The fourth is Lightning. That's Apple's own connector. It's small and reversible, and it was used on iPhones and iPads for about ten years. Then, in twenty twenty three, the iPhone switched to USB-C. But Lightning devices are still everywhere, and still on the exam.

Recap of part one. USB-C is reversible, and carries data, power, and video. Micro USB and mini USB are older, and only fit one way. Lightning is Apple's reversible connector, found on older iPhones and iPads.

[pause 1]

Part two. Wireless connections.

First, Bluetooth. Bluetooth creates a personal area network, or PAN. It connects devices over a short distance, usually up to about ten meters. Think headsets, keyboards, and smart watches.

Second, NFC, or near field communication. NFC only works over a few centimeters. You have to tap, or almost touch. That short range is a security feature. It's used for contactless payments, for tapping a badge to open a door, and for quickly pairing two devices.

Third, tethering, and the mobile hotspot. Both share a phone's cellular data connection with other devices. Tethering usually means sharing it over a USB cable or Bluetooth. A hotspot means the phone acts as a small Wi-Fi access point, so a laptop or tablet can join it like any other wireless network.

A hotspot is great when there's no Wi-Fi. But keep two things in mind. It drains the phone's battery quickly. And every byte the laptop uses comes out of the phone's data plan.

Recap of part two. Bluetooth is a short range personal area network, up to about ten meters. NFC works over a few centimeters, for payments, badges, and pairing. Tethering and hotspots share the phone's cellular data with other devices.

[pause 1]

Part three. Accessories and docking stations.

The exam lists a handful of mobile accessories. A stylus, which is a pen for touch screens. Headsets, and speakers. A webcam. And input devices like a trackpad, a drawing pad, or a track point. A trackpad is an external touchpad. A drawing pad is a tablet you draw on with a pen. And a track point is the small pointing stick in the middle of some laptop keyboards.

Now, two terms the exam likes to compare. A port replicator, and a docking station.

A port replicator does what the name says. It copies the laptop's ports onto a box on your desk. You plug your monitor, keyboard, and network cable into the box once, and then connect the laptop to it.

A docking station does that, and more. It usually charges the laptop. It often adds ports the laptop doesn't have, like extra display outputs. And bigger docking stations can add things like expansion slots, or drive bays.

Today, the line between them is blurry. A modern USB-C or Thunderbolt dock connects with one cable. That one cable charges the laptop, and carries the monitors, the wired network, and the USB devices. For the exam, remember this. A port replicator copies ports. A docking station adds features, like charging and extra ports.

Recap of part three. Accessories include styluses, headsets, speakers, webcams, trackpads, drawing pads, and track points. A port replicator copies the laptop's ports. A docking station adds features, like charging and extra ports, often through a single USB-C cable.

Part four. Cellular data, and SIM cards.

Phones connect to the internet in two ways. Over Wi-Fi, or over the cellular network. In the settings, you can turn each one on or off. Airplane mode turns off all the radios at once. You can then switch Wi-Fi and Bluetooth back on, while cellular stays off.

The cellular generations you'll see are 3G, 4G, and 5G. 3G is old and slow, and most carriers have already shut it down. 4G, also called LTE, is the everyday standard. 5G is the newest and fastest.

To use a cellular network, the phone needs a SIM. SIM stands for subscriber identity module. The SIM identifies you to the carrier. It's what ties the phone to your account and your phone number. The traditional SIM is a tiny card, in a small tray on the side of the phone. Move the card to another phone, and your number moves with it.

The newer option is the eSIM, or embedded SIM. It's a chip built into the phone, so there's no card to swap. Instead, the carrier sends a profile to the phone, usually when you scan a QR code, or use the carrier's app. One eSIM can store several profiles. That makes it easy to add a second number, or a travel plan abroad. Some new phones have no SIM tray at all.

Recap of part four. Phones use Wi-Fi or cellular data, and airplane mode turns off all the radios. 3G is retired, 4G is common, and 5G is the fastest. A SIM identifies the subscriber to the carrier. An eSIM is built in, and gets its profile from a QR code or the carrier's app.

Part five. Bluetooth pairing, and location services.

Before two Bluetooth devices can talk, they have to be paired. The exam lists the steps, so here they are, in order.

Step one. Enable Bluetooth on the phone or laptop.

Step two. Enable pairing on the accessory. This is often called pairing mode. On a headset, you might hold the power button until a light flashes.

Step three. Find the device. On the phone, the accessory should now show up in the list of nearby devices. Select it.

Step four. Enter the PIN code, if one is asked for. Older accessories use a fixed code, often four zeros, or one two three four. Newer devices show a code on both screens, and you just confirm that they match.

Step five. Test connectivity. Play some audio, or type a few keys.

The most common problem? The accessory isn't in pairing mode, so it never shows up in the list.

Now, location services. A phone can find its location in two main ways. The first is GPS, the global positioning system. The phone receives signals from satellites. GPS works with no cellular signal at all, but it needs a clear view of the sky, so it struggles indoors. The second is cellular location services. The phone estimates its position from nearby cell towers, and often from nearby Wi-Fi networks too. That works indoors, but it's less precise.

Phones let you turn location services on or off, and choose which apps may use your location. That's an important privacy setting.

Recap of part five. To pair Bluetooth, enable Bluetooth, enable pairing mode, find the device, enter or confirm the PIN, and test. GPS uses satellites, and needs a view of the sky. Cellular location uses nearby towers and Wi-Fi networks.

Part six. Mobile device management, and sync.

Companies with hundreds of phones can't set each one up by hand. So they use mobile device management, or MDM. MDM software lets the IT team manage every enrolled phone and tablet from one central console.

The exam splits this into three areas.

First, device configurations. A corporate device is owned by the company, so IT can control all of it. A BYOD device, which stands for bring your own device, belongs to the employee. Here, MDM keeps work and personal data apart, often in a separate work profile. If the employee leaves, or loses the phone, IT can wipe just the work data, and leave the personal photos alone.

Second, policy enforcement. MDM can require a screen lock with a strong passcode, require encryption, require a minimum OS version, and block risky apps. If a device breaks the rules, MDM can block it from company email until it's fixed. And if a phone is lost, MDM can lock it, or wipe it remotely.

Third, corporate applications. MDM can push work apps to devices automatically.

Now, synchronization. Sync keeps the same data on all of a user's devices, through an account like Microsoft 365, Google, or iCloud. The exam names the things to sync. Mail. Calendar. Contacts. And cloud storage, for files and photos.

One catch. Recognizing data caps. Many cellular plans have a monthly data limit. Syncing large files or photo libraries over cellular can use it up fast. After that, the carrier may slow the connection, or charge extra. The fix is simple. Set large syncs and backups to use Wi-Fi only.

Recap of part six. MDM manages phones centrally. Corporate devices are fully managed. BYOD devices keep work data separate, so it can be wiped on its own. MDM enforces policies like screen locks and encryption, and pushes corporate apps. Sync keeps mail, calendar, contacts, and files the same everywhere. And watch the data cap, by syncing big files over Wi-Fi.

The answer. Make sure the headset is in pairing mode. Then find it in the phone's list, enter or confirm the PIN, and test the audio.
