Part one. Before you open a laptop.

A desktop is built from standard parts, with plenty of room inside. A laptop is packed tight. Many of its parts are made for that one model. Some are soldered to the motherboard, so they can't be replaced at all.

So, rule one. Get the manufacturer's service manual. It tells you which parts can be replaced, and how to remove them.

Rule two. Remove all power. Shut the laptop down, and unplug the adapter. Then disconnect the battery. On most modern laptops, the battery is inside, so you unplug its cable as soon as the bottom cover is off.

Rule three. Protect against electrostatic discharge, or ESD. Wear an ESD wrist strap.

Rule four. Stay organized. Laptop screws come in different lengths, and a long screw in the wrong hole can damage the motherboard. Keep them in order, or take photos as you go. And pry the case open with plastic tools, called spudgers, not metal ones.

Recap of part one. Read the service manual. Remove all power, including the battery. Use an ESD strap. Keep your screws organized, and use plastic tools.

Part two. Replacing memory.

Laptops use a smaller memory module than desktops. It's called a SODIMM. That's short for small outline dual inline memory module. Desktops use a full size DIMM. The two are not interchangeable.

The generation has to match, too. Current laptops use DDR4 or DDR5. Each one has its notch in a different place, so a DDR4 module won't fit a DDR5 slot. Check the manual for the supported type, the speed, and the maximum capacity.

Here's a big exam point. On many thin laptops, the memory is soldered to the motherboard. There's no slot. So it can't be upgraded.

To install a SODIMM, slide it into the slot at an angle, about thirty degrees. Then press it down flat, until the clips on each side snap into place. Finally, power on, and check that the new total shows up.

Recap of part two. Laptops use SODIMMs, not DIMMs. Match the generation, like DDR4 or DDR5. Insert at an angle, then press down until the clips lock. And soldered memory can't be upgraded.

Part three. Replacing storage.

Laptops use two main kinds of drive. The first is the two and a half inch drive. That can be a hard disk drive, or HDD, with spinning platters. Or a solid state drive, or SSD, in the same shape. Both connect with SATA.

The second kind is M.2. An M.2 drive is a small card, about the size of a stick of gum, that screws flat onto the motherboard.

Here's the trap. M.2 is only the shape, the form factor. It doesn't say how the drive talks to the computer. An M.2 drive can use SATA. Or it can use NVMe, which runs over PCIe, and is several times faster. Some slots support only one of the two. Put an NVMe drive in a slot that only supports SATA, and the laptop won't see it.

So check the manual, and check the keying. The notches on the edge of the card are called keys. An NVMe drive usually has one notch, the M key. An M.2 SATA drive usually has two, the B key and the M key. You may also see mSATA. That's an older SATA card that came before M.2.

The most common upgrade of all is replacing an old hard drive with an SSD, which has no moving parts and is much faster.

Moving the user's files and programs to the new drive is called migration. You have two choices. One, clone the old drive. Cloning software copies everything to the new drive, often through a USB to SATA adapter. Two, do a clean install. Install a fresh operating system, reinstall the programs, and restore the user's data from a backup. Either way, back up first.

Recap of part three. Two and a half inch drives use SATA. M.2 is a form factor, and an M.2 drive can be SATA or NVMe. NVMe is much faster, but the slot must support it. To migrate, clone the drive, or do a clean install and restore the data.

Part four. The battery and the keyboard.

Laptop batteries are lithium ion, or lithium polymer, and they wear out. Each full charge and discharge is called a cycle. After a few hundred cycles, a battery holds noticeably less charge. Windows can create a battery report that shows how much capacity is left. When you replace a battery, use the manufacturer's part, or an approved equivalent.

Now, the most important battery warning. Swelling. Lithium batteries can swell as they age, or after damage. The signs? The case bulges. The touchpad gets pushed up, and stops clicking. Or the laptop no longer sits flat on the desk.

A swollen battery is a fire risk. Stop using the laptop, and don't charge it. Don't puncture the battery, or press on it. Remove it carefully, and replace it. Then recycle it, following local regulations and the safety data sheet. Never put it in the regular trash.

Now, the keyboard. A single sticky key can often be cleaned with compressed air. Otherwise, you replace the keyboard.

On some laptops, the keyboard lifts out from the top. You disconnect its ribbon cable, which often uses a ZIF connector. ZIF stands for zero insertion force. You flip up a small latch, and the cable slides out freely. Never yank it. On many modern laptops, though, the keyboard is riveted to the top case, also called the palm rest. Then you replace the whole top case.

Recap of part four. Batteries wear out over many charge cycles. A swollen battery is a fire risk. Stop using it, replace it, and recycle it. Keyboards connect with a ribbon cable, often through a ZIF connector.

Part five. Laptop displays.

Most laptops use an LCD, a liquid crystal display. Liquid crystals make no light of their own. They act like tiny shutters, in front of a backlight.

There are three LCD panel types to know. TN, twisted nematic, is cheap and fast. But colors fade and shift when you view it from an angle. IPS, in plane switching, has the best color accuracy, and wide viewing angles. It's the choice for photo and design work. VA, vertical alignment, sits in the middle. Its strength is contrast, with deeper blacks.

Now, backlights. Old laptops used a CCFL backlight. That's a cold cathode fluorescent lamp. It needs AC power, so these laptops have an inverter, which converts DC power into AC for the lamp. Modern laptops use LED backlights instead, with no inverter. A newer version, mini LED, uses thousands of tiny LEDs in separate zones. Each zone can dim on its own, for much better contrast.

Then there's OLED. That stands for organic light emitting diode. Every pixel makes its own light, so there's no backlight at all. A black pixel is simply off, which gives perfect blacks. The drawback is burn in. A fixed image, left on screen for a very long time, can leave a faint ghost.

Here's a classic exam scenario. The screen is very dim. But with a flashlight, you can faintly see the image. So the panel works, but the backlight doesn't. On an old CCFL laptop, suspect the inverter.

Four display attributes to know. Resolution is the number of pixels, like nineteen twenty by ten eighty. Refresh rate, in hertz, is how many times per second the image is redrawn. Pixel density is how tightly the pixels are packed. And color gamut is the range of colors the screen can show.

The lid holds more than the screen. It holds the Wi-Fi antennas. Thin antenna cables run from the wireless card, which is usually a small M.2 card, up to the top of the lid. So if the Wi-Fi gets weak right after a screen replacement, check the antenna cables. The lid also holds the webcam and the microphone. And on a touch screen, it holds the digitizer. That's the layer that turns touch into input. If the picture is fine, but touch doesn't work, suspect the digitizer.

Recap of part five. LCDs need a backlight. TN is cheap, with poor viewing angles. IPS has the best color. VA has the best contrast. CCFL backlights need an inverter. LED and mini LED don't. OLED needs no backlight, but can burn in. And the lid holds the Wi-Fi antennas, the webcam, the microphone, and the digitizer.

Part six. Biometrics.

Biometrics means identifying a person by a physical feature. It's something you are. Laptops offer two common options.

The first is a fingerprint reader, often built into the power button. The user enrolls a finger once, and then a touch signs them in.

The second is facial recognition. On Windows, this is part of Windows Hello. It uses an infrared, or IR, camera next to the webcam. Infrared works in the dark, and is much harder to fool with a photo. With either option, a PIN is set up as the backup.

Don't confuse these with an NFC scanner. NFC stands for near field communication, and it works over a few centimeters. A user can tap a badge, or a smart card, on the palm rest to sign in. But a badge is something you have, not something you are.

Recap of part six. Fingerprint readers and facial recognition are biometrics, something you are. Windows Hello face sign in uses an IR camera, with a PIN as the backup. And an NFC badge is something you have.
